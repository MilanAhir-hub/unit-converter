Until recently, if you wanted to know the exact weight of a kilogram, you had to refer to a physical object: a polished cylinder of platinum-iridium kept under lock and key in Sèvres, France. This object was known affectionately as 'Le Grand K'.

### The Metal Masterpiece

Constructed in 1889, the International Prototype of the Kilogram (IPK), or Le Grand K, was protected by three nested glass bell jars in a highly secure underground vault. Official copies were sent to nations around the globe to calibrate their own national standards. Every 40 years, Le Grand K was taken out and weighed against its official copies to ensure consistency.

### The Mysterious Weight Loss

During a routine comparison in 1989, scientists discovered a alarming discrepancy: Le Grand K and its official copies were drifting apart in weight by about 50 micrograms (roughly the mass of a single eyelash). No one knew if Le Grand K was losing mass due to cleaning, or if the copies were absorbing contaminants from the air. In metrology, a standard that changes over time is unacceptable.

### Redefining Mass via Planck's Constant

In November 2018, representatives from over 60 countries voted unanimously to redefine the kilogram using a fundamental constant of nature: Planck's constant. By using a device called a Kibble balance, scientists can now define mass by measuring the electromagnetic force required to balance a physical weight, linking mechanical power to electrical power with extreme quantum precision.

### Conclusion

Since May 20, 2019, the physical cylinder in Paris is no longer the absolute standard. The kilogram is now defined quantum-mechanically, ensuring it remains stable for all time, even if we travel to other planets.
