To convert Celsius to Fahrenheit, multiply the temperature by **1.8** and then add **32**. For example, 20°C is 68°F ((20 × 1.8) + 32 = 68).

Temperature is one of the few measurements where the two main systems don't just use different units—they start at different points and use different scales. While most of the world uses Celsius, the United States and a few other territories stick with Fahrenheit. Understanding the math between them is essential for travelers, scientists, and anyone who enjoys international cooking.

### The Physics of the Formula

To understand the formula `°F = (°C × 9/5) + 32`, we have to look at how the two scales were created.

1.  **The Starting Point:** The Celsius scale starts at the freezing point of water (0°C). The Fahrenheit scale starts its "water" measurements at 32°F. This is why we **add 32** at the end of the conversion.
2.  **The Scale Size:** In Celsius, there are exactly 100 degrees between freezing (0°) and boiling (100°). In Fahrenheit, there are 180 degrees between freezing (32°) and boiling (212°).
3.  **The Ratio:** The ratio of the scales is 180/100, which simplifies to **1.8** or **9/5**. This is why we multiply by 1.8 before adding the offset.

### Mental Math for Travelers

When you're looking at a weather forecast in a foreign country, you probably don't want to pull out a calculator. Here is the "Double and Add 30" shortcut:

*   **Step 1:** Double the Celsius number.
*   **Step 2:** Add 30.

*Example (20°C):* 20 × 2 = 40. 40 + 30 = **70°F**. (Actual: 68°F).
*Example (30°C):* 30 × 2 = 60. 60 + 30 = **90°F**. (Actual: 86°F).

This shortcut gets less accurate as the temperature goes up, but for standard weather temperatures, it's usually within 2 to 4 degrees—perfect for deciding whether to wear a sweater!

### Why Do We Still Use Two Systems?

The Celsius scale (also known as Centigrade) is preferred in scientific circles because it is based on the properties of water and fits perfectly into the metric system. 

Fahrenheit supporters, however, argue that their scale is more "human-centric" for weather. In most inhabited parts of the world, the outdoor temperature stays between 0°F and 100°F. Furthermore, because Fahrenheit degrees are smaller (1.8 Fahrenheit degrees fit into 1 Celsius degree), it allows for more precise weather reporting without using decimals.

### Conclusion

Whether you think in "0 is freezing" or "32 is freezing," the ability to convert between the two scales is a vital skill. By understanding that the difference is a combination of a **scale ratio (1.8)** and an **offset (32)**, the formula becomes much easier to remember.
