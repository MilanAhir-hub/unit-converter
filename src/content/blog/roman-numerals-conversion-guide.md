They appear on the faces of old clocks, in the credits of movies, and at the end of every Super Bowl. **Roman Numerals** are a mathematical relic of one of history's greatest empires. While we have replaced them with the more efficient Hindu-Arabic system (0-9), learning to read them is a fun and culturally important skill.

### 1. The Seven Symbols

The entire Roman system is built from just seven capital letters:
*   **I** = 1
*   **V** = 5
*   **X** = 10
*   **L** = 50
*   **C** = 100
*   **D** = 500
*   **M** = 1,000

### 2. The Rule of Addition

When symbols are placed after a larger symbol, you add them.
*   **VI:** 5 + 1 = 6
*   **XV:** 10 + 5 = 15
*   **CLXX:** 100 + 50 + 10 + 10 = 170

### 3. The Rule of Subtraction

When a smaller symbol is placed **before** a larger one, you subtract it. This was done to avoid having more than three of the same symbols in a row (e.g., they used IV instead of IIII).
*   **IV:** 5 - 1 = 4
*   **IX:** 10 - 1 = 9
*   **XC:** 100 - 10 = 90
*   **CM:** 1,000 - 100 = 900

### 4. Converting Large Numbers

To convert a complex year like **1994** into Roman Numerals, break it down:
1.  **1,000:** M
2.  **900:** CM
3.  **90:** XC
4.  **4:** IV
**Result:** MCMXCIV.

### 5. Why We Still Use Them

If Roman Numerals are so hard to use for complex math (try multiplying MCMXCIV by XVII without a calculator!), why are they still around?
*   **Aesthetics:** They look stately and important. This is why they are used on monuments, gravestones, and book prefaces.
*   **Clarity:** In law and science, they are used to distinguish sub-points (i, ii, iii, iv) from primary numbers (1, 2, 3, 4).
*   **Tradition:** From the Olympic Games to monarchs (Queen Elizabeth II), they connect us to a sense of historical continuity.

### Conclusion

Roman Numerals are a beautiful bridge to our classical past. By mastering the seven symbols and the rules of addition and subtraction, you can decode the dates on ancient buildings and the chapters of classic literature with ease.
