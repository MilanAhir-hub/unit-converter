In a world that is 95% metric, the cockpit of a modern airliner remains a bastion of traditional units. Regardless of whether they are flying over Paris, Tokyo, or New York, pilots almost universally communicate in **feet** for altitude and **knots** for speed. This isn't just tradition; it's a critical safety standard that prevents mid-air collisions.

### The Problem of Vertical Separation

In the sky, you don't have lanes. To keep planes from hitting each other, air traffic controllers use **Vertical Separation**. 
By international agreement, planes flying in the same direction must be separated by at least **1,000 feet**. 
*   **Why not meters?** A 1,000-foot gap is about 300 meters. If the world used meters, the standard might be "300m separation." However, because a foot is a smaller unit than a meter, the dials in the cockpit move more noticeably, giving pilots finer control over their exact height.

### Knots and the Nautical Mile

As we've explored in other guides, a **knot** is one nautical mile per hour. Because a nautical mile is exactly one minute of latitude, it is perfectly synced with the maps and coordinates used for global navigation. 

If a pilot is flying at **480 knots**, they are crossing **8 degrees of latitude** every hour. This simple mathematical relationship makes it much easier to estimate arrival times and fuel consumption across vast, featureless oceans where there are no landmarks to look at.

### The Temperature and Pressure Exception

While altitude and speed are traditional, other parts of the cockpit are increasingly metric. 
*   **Temperature:** Almost all modern aircraft displays show outside air temperature in **Celsius**. This is vital for calculating engine performance and the risk of icing on the wings.
*   **Pressure:** This is where it gets confusing. In the US, pilots use **Inches of Mercury (inHg)** for pressure. In Europe and most of the rest of the world, they use **Hectopascals (hPa)**. A pilot flying from London to New York has to switch their thinking—and their instruments—mid-flight.

### Mach: The High-Speed Unit

At high altitudes, "speed" becomes a relative concept because the speed of sound changes with temperature. This is why jet pilots switch their focus from knots to **Mach**. 
*   **Mach 1.0** is the speed of sound.
*   Commercial jets usually cruise at **Mach 0.80 to 0.85**. 

Measuring in Mach ensures that the pilot doesn't accidentally fly too fast and create dangerous shockwaves on the wings that could lead to a loss of control.

### Conclusion

The aviation measurement system is a "hybrid" built for survival. While it might seem odd to use feet and knots in the 21st century, these units provide a universal language that keeps the skies organized. In a world where a small misunderstanding can lead to tragedy, the consistency of the "Aviation System" is its greatest strength.
