When you look at a car's fuel efficiency in the United States, you see **Miles Per Gallon (MPG)**. Higher is better. But if you look at a car in Europe or Canada, you see **L/100km (Liters per 100 Kilometers)**. In this case, **lower is better**. This fundamental difference in "thinking" makes it one of the most confusing conversions in the world of measurement.

### Efficiency vs. Consumption

*   **MPG (Efficiency):** This asks: "I have one gallon of gas; how far can I go?"
*   **L/100km (Consumption):** This asks: "I want to go 100km; how much gas will I need?"

Environmentalists often prefer the **L/100km** measurement because it shows the "cost" of the journey more clearly. If you improve a car from 10 L/100km to 8 L/100km, you've saved exactly 2 liters of gas for every 100km driven. 

### The Mathematical Flip

Because these units are inverses of each other (one is distance/fuel, the other is fuel/distance), you can't just multiply by a simple factor. You have to divide.

**The Golden Number: 235.21**

*   **To get L/100km:** `235.21 / MPG = L/100km`
*   **To get MPG:** `235.21 / L/100km = MPG`

*Example:* If your car gets **30 MPG**:
`235.21 / 30 = 7.84 L/100km`.

### The UK Trap: Imperial Gallons

To make matters worse, the United Kingdom still uses "MPG" for its cars, but their "Gallon" is 20% larger than the US Gallon. 
*   **1 US Gallon:** 3.785 Liters.
*   **1 Imperial Gallon:** 4.546 Liters.

This means a car that gets **30 MPG in London** would only get about **25 MPG in New York**, even though the car is performing exactly the same! When reading international car reviews, always check which "MPG" they are using.

### Mental Math Shortcuts

It's hard to divide by 235.21 in your head. Try these anchor points:
*   **23.5 MPG** is exactly **10 L/100km**.
*   **47 MPG** is exactly **5 L/100km**.
*   If the L/100km is around **8**, you're getting roughly **30 MPG**.

### Why Consumption (L/100km) is the Future

Many experts believe the "L/100km" model is better for public policy. It prevents the "MPG Illusion." People often think that improving a car from 10 MPG to 20 MPG is the same as improving one from 30 MPG to 40 MPG. 
In reality, the jump from 10 to 20 MPG saves **vastly more fuel** than the jump from 30 to 40. Measuring in consumption makes these savings obvious and transparent.

### Conclusion

Whether you prefer the efficiency of MPG or the consumption logic of L/100km, understanding the relationship between the two is vital for comparing cars and calculating travel costs. Keep the number **235** in mind, and you'll always be able to translate the data at the pump.
