The ocean is a vast, featureless place. To navigate it successfully, sailors for centuries have developed a unique language of measurement. While modern GPS has made things easier, the traditional units of the sea—knots, fathoms, and nautical miles—remain the global standard for maritime safety and communication.

### 1. Depth: The Fathom

When you're at sea, the most important measurement isn't how far you've gone, but how much water is under your boat.
*   **1 Fathom = 6 Feet.**
*   **History:** Before electronic "sounders," sailors would drop a lead weight on a rope into the water. To pull it back up, they would stretch the rope between their arms. One "reach" was roughly six feet, or one fathom.

If a chart says "5 fathoms," you know you have 30 feet of water. For a large ship, that might be dangerously shallow!

### 2. Distance: The Nautical Mile

As we've discussed in other guides, the **nautical mile (1,852 meters)** is the standard for distance at sea. Because it is tied to the Earth's latitude, it allows sailors to plot their course directly onto a chart without needing a complex conversion tool.

### 3. Speed: The Knot

Speed at sea is always given in **knots** (nautical miles per hour). 
*   **Fun Fact:** A ship doing 20 knots is traveling about 23 MPH.
*   **The Log-Line:** Historically, a "log" was a piece of wood tossed overboard. A rope with knots tied at specific intervals (usually every 47 feet, 3 inches) was allowed to run out for 28 seconds. The number of knots that passed through the sailor's hands was their speed.

### 4. Coastal Units: Cables and Leagues

For smaller distances, especially near the shore, you might hear:
*   **Cable:** 0.1 nautical miles (about 185 meters). Used for measuring the distance between ships or to an anchor point.
*   **League:** 3 nautical miles. Historically, this was the distance a person could see to the horizon from a ship's deck, and it often defined the "territorial waters" of a country.

### 5. Bearings: Degrees and Points

A compass is divided into **360 degrees**. However, old salts might still refer to the "32 points" of the compass (like "North-North-East"). In modern navigation, we always use three digits for a bearing (e.g., "045 degrees") to prevent confusion over the radio.

### Conclusion

Maritime measurements are born of the physical reality of the sea. They were designed to be used with the tools available: your arms, a piece of rope, and the stars. Even in our high-tech world, these units keep us connected to the ancient history of exploration and the timeless geometry of our planet.
