Temperature is one of the most difficult physical quantities to measure because it cannot be seen directly. The two primary scales used in the world today—Fahrenheit and Celsius—approached this puzzle from two entirely different directions.

### Fahrenheit: The Mercury Pioneer

In the early 1700s, German physicist Daniel Gabriel Fahrenheit invented the first reliable mercury-in-glass thermometer. To calibrate it, he needed a reproducible scale. He established three reference points: 0°F was the temperature of an equal mixture of ice, water, and ammonium chloride salt (the coldest temperature he could reliably reproduce in his lab). 32°F was the freezing point of pure water, and 96°F was his estimate of human body temperature (calibrated using his wife's armpit).

### Celsius and the Inverted Centigrade Scale

In 1742, Swedish astronomer Anders Celsius proposed a simpler, decimal-based alternative: the Centigrade scale. He chose the freezing and boiling points of pure water at sea level as his reference points. However, Celsius originally inverted the scale: 0°C was designated as the boiling point of water, and 100°C was the freezing point! A year after Celsius's death, famous botanist Carl Linnaeus led a push to reverse the scale to its modern configuration (0°C freezing, 100°C boiling).

### Why Two Scales Exist Today

While Celsius became the standard for science and international commerce, the British Empire adopted Fahrenheit. Although the UK eventually converted to Celsius, the United States retained Fahrenheit for daily meteorological reporting. Proponents of Fahrenheit argue that the scale is superior for weather reporting because it has finer temperature divisions (a 1°F change is smaller than a 1°C change) and ranges from 0 to 100 in typical human climates.

### Conclusion

Whether you use Fahrenheit or Celsius, both scales paved the way for modern thermodynamics and the absolute Kelvin scale used in advanced scientific research today.
