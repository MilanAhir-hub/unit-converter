In 1792, amidst the chaos of the French Revolution, the National Assembly decided to discard the confusing, corrupt, and localized systems of measurement that plagued trade. Their solution was the 'metre'—a universal standard of length based on the dimensions of the Earth itself.

### A World of 250,000 Different Units

Before the metric system, Europe was a mess of local measurements. A 'foot' in Paris was different from a 'foot' in Bordeaux, which was different from a 'foot' in London. It was estimated that France alone had over 250,000 different units of weight and measure. This system allowed merchants to cheat consumers, collection agencies to cheat farmers, and made scientific collaboration almost impossible. French reformers wanted a system 'for all people, for all time.'

### Measuring the Earth: The Grand Meridian

To base the meter on a natural constant, scientists defined it as one ten-millionth of the distance from the equator to the North Pole, along the meridian passing through Paris. To determine this length with extreme precision, two astronomers—Pierre Méchain and Jean-Baptiste Delambre—were commissioned to measure the meridian arc between Dunkirk and Barcelona. Méchain went south, while Delambre went north.

### Arrests, War, and Astronomical Precision

The expedition was supposed to take two years; it took seven. As the French Revolution entered the Reign of Terror, both astronomers were repeatedly arrested as suspected spies for pointing telescope-like instruments (repeating circles) from church towers. Méchain caught malaria and suffered severe injuries, while Spain went to war with France, trapping him behind enemy lines. Despite these trials, their mathematical calculations were accurate to within fractions of a millimeter.

### The Platinum Standard

Upon their return, the calculated length of the meridian was used to construct the 'Mètre des Archives'—a physical bar made of pure platinum. This bar served as the official definition of the meter for nearly a century until technology advanced to define the meter in terms of the speed of light.

### Conclusion

Today, the meter is defined as the distance traveled by light in a vacuum during a time interval of 1/299,792,458 of a second. But we owe its existence to two astronomers who risked their lives to measure the Earth.
